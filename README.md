1. Data Inventory and Business Context
All four datasets used in this project were provided directly by Vise. Our team did not bring in any external, public, or web-scraped data sources. Three of the datasets, the ETF price data, the dividend data, and the stock splits data — were used as direct inputs to build and power the portfolio engine. The fourth, the model portfolio file, was used as a reference to understand the business context: what kinds of securities Vise manages, how portfolios are structured, and how target allocations are defined in practice. It was not a computational input to the engine.
The engine our team built is designed to support automated portfolio rebalancing for financial advisors on the Vise platform. The primary objective is to determine, for any given client account, what trades need to be made to bring actual holdings in line with a target model allocation, while accounting for current market prices, income from dividends, and historical price adjustments due to corporate actions like stock splits. Each dataset plays a specific role in enabling this workflow.
Client-Provided Datasets Used to Build the Engine
ETF Price Dataset (price_csv)
Vise constructs portfolios almost entirely from Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) — diversified, low-cost funds traded on public exchanges that track indices or asset classes like US bonds, international equities, or alternatives. To make any rebalancing decision, the engine needs to know the current and historical price of every ETF it might hold or trade. The ETF price dataset is what provides that foundation. It contains structured daily market trading data for U.S.-listed ETFs, capturing how each fund closed, what its bid and ask prices were, how much volume it traded, and what exchange it was listed on. For someone unfamiliar with Vise, this is the historical market ledger the engine reads to understand what securities are worth, how actively they trade, and how expensive they are to buy or sell.
Each row represents one ETF on one specific trading date. This is daily security-level granularity — the dataset is a time series, not a snapshot, and can be used to track price trends, volatility, and liquidity patterns over time. The dataset contains approximately 305,000 records across 24 fields. Based on the record volume and the number of ETFs covered, the dataset spans multiple years of daily trading history, likely from the mid-2010s through early 2026, providing the depth needed for back-testing and historical performance analysis.
The target variable the engine optimizes against is PRICECLOSE (float), the end-of-day market value used to calculate current portfolio worth and determine how far a holding has drifted from its target weight. The primary supporting features are VOLUME (integer), which is a liquidity proxy indicating how easily a position can be entered or exited without moving the market; PRICEBID (float) and PRICEASK (float), which define the bid-ask spread and allow the engine to estimate realistic round-trip transaction costs; and PRICEDATE (datetime), the time dimension that sequences all records into a usable series. Each record is linked to a specific security through a numeric trading item identifier, which is the shared key connecting this dataset to the dividend and splits tables. The data is fully structured and tabular with no unstructured or semi-structured fields.
Dividend Dataset
ETFs regularly distribute income to their shareholders in the form of dividends, cash payments made on a per-share basis, typically on a monthly or quarterly schedule. Vise manages conservative, income-oriented portfolios where many of the holdings, such as bond ETFs and multi-asset funds, are specifically chosen because they generate regular cash distributions. For the engine to accurately simulate how a portfolio grows and how cash flows in and out of an account over time, it must account for every dividend payment. Without this data, the engine would understate total returns and misrepresent account cash balances whenever an income-generating security is held.
Each row represents a single dividend payment event for a single security, for example, one ETF paying out a specific dollar amount per share on a specific date. The granularity is event-level at the security-payment level, not a daily or monthly summary. The dataset contains over 10,000 records based on the dividend ID (integer) range visible in the extract (IDs in the 10,629,000 to 10,631,000 range), indicating the full database spans a large number of securities across many years of payment history. The visible records in our extract cover late January through early February 2026, and the ID range strongly suggests the full dataset extends several years back. The data is fully structured and tabular.
The key variable used by the engine is DIVAMOUNT (float), the per-share dollar value of the payment, which is multiplied by shares held to compute total income received. EXDATE (datetime) is equally critical because it is the ex-dividend date — the cutoff by which a shareholder must own the security to be eligible for the payment — and is what the engine uses to determine whether a given account qualifies for a distribution on any given day. PAYDATE (datetime) records when cash is actually transferred. NETAMOUNT (float) and GROSSAMOUNT (float) support after-tax performance reporting where applicable. The security is identified by a numeric trading item ID that joins directly to the price and splits datasets.
Stock Splits Dataset
From time to time, the funds and securities in a portfolio undergo stock splits — corporate actions where existing shares are divided into a greater number of new shares. In a standard 2-for-1 split, an investor ends up with twice the shares at half the price per share, with no change in total position value. The reverse can also happen in what is called a reverse split. For a portfolio engine, these events matter because any calculation comparing prices or returns across a split date will produce misleading results if the adjustment is not made. A security that traded at 00 before a 2-for-1 split and at 00 afterward has not lost half its value — but unadjusted price data would make it look exactly that way, corrupting back-testing results and historical return calculations.
Each row represents one split event for one security on one effective date. The granularity is event-level at the security-split level. The dataset contains approximately 2,600 or more records based on the split ID (integer) range in the extract (IDs from roughly 2,650,000 to 2,671,000), spanning from at least March 2000 through December 2025. This gives the engine over two decades of corporate action history to draw from when reconstructing adjusted historical price series, which is necessary for any security with a long trading record. The data is fully structured and tabular.
The key fields for the engine are EXDATE (datetime), the effective date from which the adjusted share count and price apply and the anchor point for all retroactive price corrections; RATE (float), the split ratio that is applied as a multiplier to historical share counts and as a divisor to historical prices; APPLIEDFLAG (binary), a binary indicator showing whether the source system has already incorporated the adjustment, which prevents the engine from double-counting; and SPLITTYPEID (integer), which distinguishes forward splits from reverse splits since the two require opposite adjustment logic. The security joins to the price and dividend datasets through the same numeric trading item identifier.
Client-Provided Reference Dataset
Model Portfolio Dataset (Vise 20/80 Model)
Vise also provided a model portfolio file that our team used not as a computational input, but as a business reference to understand the environment the engine operates in. This file defines the target asset allocation for the 20/80 conservative portfolio under the “TA MM w Alts” product family — one of Vise's live investment models used by financial advisors to manage client accounts. The 20/80 label reflects the approximate split between growth assets (roughly 20% in equities and alternatives) and income assets (roughly 80% in fixed income). It is a representative example of the kind of model the engine is designed to support.
Each row represents one target security holding within the model, specifying the ticker (string), the intended allocation weight (float), the asset class it belongs (string) to, and the turnover amount (float) needed to bring an actual portfolio in line with the target. The dataset is a point-in-time snapshot as of May 20, 2025, not a time series , and contains approximately 20 holdings across around 10 fields. It is fully structured and tabular. The ticker field here is the human-readable security identifier, while the engine datasets use a numeric trading item ID for the same securities. Mapping between the two was a key data preparation step our team addressed before integrating all four sources.
Reviewing this dataset gave our team the business framing needed to interpret the other three datasets correctly. It clarified what kinds of securities the engine will encounter, how Vise categorizes holdings by asset class, and what a properly rebalanced portfolio actually looks like as an end state. That context directly shaped the design decisions we made in building the engine.
Data Relationships
All three engine datasets are linked through a shared numeric security identifier, the trading item ID (integer). The ETF price dataset defines the universe of securities and provides the daily market record for each one. The dividend dataset attaches income event records to those same securities over time, and the splits dataset attaches corporate action adjustment records. The relationship between the price dataset and each of the other two is one-to-many: one security in the price data can have many dividend payment records distributed across its payment history and many split events accumulated over its lifetime. The engine must join all three sources by security ID and process events in chronological order to produce accurate adjusted price series and complete income-aware portfolio simulations. The model portfolio reference dataset sits outside this join structure but provided the interpretive framework that informed how the engine logic was designed and what outputs matter to the business.

2. Data Quality Assessment
Overall, the data quality is very strong. Our sponsor, Brandt, provided us all the csv and excel files we need and these data files are very clean and organized with a few missing/NaN values, most of which are for variables we don’t need to use. The fields that matter most for the engine we have built so far—ticker, date, security ID, and daily price—are present and usable. In the current code, the core price file is cleaned before analysis by converting PRICEDATE to a true date field, filtering to valid TRADINGITEMSTATUSID values, converting the selected price field to numeric, dropping rows where that price is missing, and standardizing ticker symbols. Because those core fields are usable, the main data issues are routine cleaning issues rather than major problems that block the project.
A minor issue is that some raw price fields are incomplete (roughly 0.5% of the entire dataset), but this has limited impact on the current engine. For the work completed so far, the engine only relies on a narrow set of price columns and uses either PRICECLOSE or PRICEMID as the main valuation input. Since the code drops rows only when the chosen price field is missing, missing values in other vendor fields do not materially affect current results. In practice, this means the price dataset is usable as long as the selected daily price series is available for the tickers and dates in the simulation window.
Another minor issue is that the raw files are not perfectly standardized, so some fields need formatting and mapping before they can be used together. The main example is date handling: both the base engine and the optimizer explicitly convert date columns into datetime format before using them. Ticker formatting also has to be standardized by trimming and uppercasing symbols. In the optimizer, the dividend file may also need a mapping step if it identifies securities by TRADINGITEMID instead of TICKERSYMBOL. This is not a flaw in the business data itself, but it is a real integration issue that required preprocessing before the files could work together reliably.
The dividend data is usable for the tax-aware module, and any missing values in non-core fields are not material right now. In the optimizer, dividends are only used to add cash or reinvest cash based on PAYDATE, DIVAMOUNT, and the security identifier. The code builds a dividend lookup using those fields after converting dates and filtering to relevant tickers. Because of that, any missing values in secondary descriptive fields do not meaningfully affect the current simulation. For the present version of the project, dividend quality only matters if it affects the event date, amount, or ticker mapping.
As a whole, the data issues are very manageable and do not prevent our rebalancing engine from working correctly. The main work has been standard data preparation: cleaning the price file, making dates usable, standardizing tickers, and aligning dividend records when the optimizer is turned on. Missing values in fields that are not used in the analysis are not material to the current version of the project. As a result, the datasets are sufficiently reliable for the current MVP and for the next phase of development.


3. Data Gaps

While the datasets Vise provided are well-structured and sufficient for building the current version of the portfolio accounting engine, several categories of data that would have added significant value to the project are either absent from the files, incomplete within them, or unavailable for reasons outside the team's control. This section documents each meaningful gap, explains why it matters for the questions the engine is designed to answer, identifies why the data is unavailable, and describes the workaround our team adopted.

Real Client Account Data and Existing Portfolio Holdings
The engine is designed to simulate how a portfolio evolves over time through trades, dividends, and tax events, but it has no access to actual client account records. There are no real holdings files, no existing cost-basis lots, and no historical position snapshots from a custodian or brokerage system. This matters because the engine's rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting logic ultimately needs to operate on real portfolios with pre-existing positions, unrealized gains, and established lot histories. Without that data, the engine cannot be validated against actual client outcomes. This data is unavailable because Vise manages real client accounts through its platform and cannot share personally identifiable financial records due to regulatory and privacy restrictions. Our team worked around this by initializing the engine with a flat cash starting balance of $100,000 and generating synthetic trade schedules using weight-based allocation logic. The engine's `build_weight_trades()` function converts target model weights into dollar-amount buy orders, simulating an initial portfolio construction from cash. This approach lets the engine run end-to-end and produce realistic outputs, but the results reflect simulated behavior rather than real client performance.

Historical Transaction Records
Closely related to the absence of client account data, the engine has no access to actual brokerage transaction confirmations, settlement records, or trade blotters. Every trade in the current engine is manually defined in a `scheduled_trades` DataFrame with hardcoded dates, share counts, and actions. This matters because a production-grade engine would need to ingest real trade data to accurately track lot-level cost basis, compute realized gains, and enforce tax rules over a client's actual trading history. Vise's transaction records are stored in internal systems tied to their custodial infrastructure and cannot be extracted or shared with an external team. Our team accepted this limitation and instead designed the engine to accept any properly formatted trade schedule as input, making it flexible enough to integrate with real transaction feeds in a future production deployment. The synthetic trades we use for testing, such as a 60/40 initial allocation followed by a partial sell on day 120, are intentionally representative of the kinds of actions the engine would process in practice.

Net Dividend Amounts (NETAMOUNT Field)
The dividend dataset includes a NETAMOUNT column that would represent the after-tax or after-withholding dividend amount per share, which would be directly useful for computing after-tax income and reinvestment amounts. However, this field is entirely empty across all 55,064 dividend records. The GROSSAMOUNT field is also partially missing, with approximately 38.7% of records blank. These fields are likely unpopulated because the source system (Capital IQ / Compustat) does not consistently capture net and gross amounts for all dividend events, particularly for ETFs where withholding structures vary by fund and investor type. Our team worked around this by relying exclusively on DIVAMOUNT, which is fully populated, and applying a flat qualified dividend tax rate of 15% in the engine's tax configuration. The after-tax dividend is computed as `DIVAMOUNT * shares_held * (1 - dividend_rate)`, and the net proceeds are reinvested through the DRIP logic. This is a simplification — real dividend taxation depends on the investor's bracket, whether the dividend is qualified or non-qualified, and whether any foreign withholding applies — but it produces a reasonable estimate for simulation purposes.

Net Asset Value (NAV Field)
The price dataset includes a NAV column that would provide the net asset value for ETFs, which can differ from the market close price due to premiums or discounts. However, NAV is missing for approximately 96.2% of all records, making it unusable as a primary valuation input. This is likely because NAV is only reported for a subset of fund-type securities in the source data and is not consistently populated across all trading items. Our team addressed this by using PRICECLOSE as the sole valuation input for all securities. For the ETFs in Vise's model portfolios, close prices closely track NAV on most trading days, so this substitution does not introduce material error in the portfolio valuation or return calculations.

Ex-Dividend Date Eligibility Logic
The dividend dataset includes both EXDATE and PAYDATE, but the engine currently processes dividends based solely on PAYDATE — it checks how many shares are held on the payment date and distributes dividends accordingly. In reality, dividend eligibility is determined by whether shares were held on the ex-dividend date, not the payment date. A shareholder who buys after the ex-date but before the pay date would not be entitled to the dividend, and the current engine does not enforce this distinction. The data itself is not the gap here — EXDATE is fully populated — but the engine's logic was simplified in this version to avoid the added complexity of tracking record-date eligibility across lot purchase dates. Our team accepted this limitation for the current MVP. In practice, the impact is small because the simulation does not involve rapid trading around ex-dates, but this is a known area for refinement in future versions.

Stock Split Integration
The splits dataset contains 186 split records with complete EXDATE and RATE fields, and the price dataset contains the raw (unadjusted) daily prices. However, the current version of the portfolio accounting engine does not incorporate split adjustments into its price series or share count logic. This matters because any historical price comparison or return calculation that spans a split date will produce incorrect results if the split is not applied — a 2-for-1 split would appear as a 50% price drop in the raw data. The split data itself is available and well-structured, but integrating it into the engine requires retroactively adjusting both the price matrix and any open lot share counts and cost bases, which adds significant complexity to the accounting logic. Our team narrowed the simulation scope to a one-year forward window (January 2025 through December 2025) using only two example securities, which reduces the likelihood of encountering a split event during the simulation period. The splits dataset remains available for integration in a future version of the engine, and the APPLIEDFLAG field in the data will help prevent double-counting when that integration occurs.

Wash Sale Rule Data
The IRS wash sale rule prohibits a taxpayer from claiming a tax loss on a security if a substantially identical security is purchased within 30 days before or after the loss sale. This rule is critical for any tax-loss harvesting strategy, which is a core part of Vise's value proposition. The engine currently supports a TAX_OPTIMAL lot disposal strategy that prioritizes selling loss lots for tax benefit, but it has no mechanism to detect or prevent wash sales. Enforcing this rule would require either a mapping of substantially identical securities or integration with the proxy lookup file that Vise provided, which maps each security to its TLH substitutes. The proxy lookup dataset does exist in the provided files and contains substitute mappings (for example, AAAU maps to GLDM as a TLH substitute), but the current engine version does not consume this file. Our team acknowledged this as a known limitation and scoped the current engine to demonstrate the mechanics of tax-aware lot disposal without full wash sale compliance. Integrating the proxy lookup and adding a 30-day lookback window for wash sale detection is a planned enhancement.

Market Holiday Calendar
The engine's daily valuation loop runs on `pd.bdate_range`, which skips weekends but does not exclude U.S. market holidays such as Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents' Day, Good Friday, or Thanksgiving. This means the engine attempts to value the portfolio on days when markets are actually closed. The impact is limited because the price matrix is forward-filled across all calendar days, so holiday dates simply carry forward the last known close price, but it does mean the engine produces valuation records for non-trading days and could theoretically schedule trades on days when execution would be impossible. A standard market holiday calendar is not included in the Vise-provided datasets because it is a reference dataset that exists in public financial data libraries rather than in a client's proprietary data. Our team accepted this limitation and relied on the forward-fill mechanism as a sufficient proxy. The engine's daily return calculations are not materially affected because the forward-filled price on a holiday produces a zero return for that day, which washes out in cumulative performance.

Benchmark Return Data
The engine computes portfolio-level cumulative return and daily return but has no benchmark return series — such as the S&P 500, the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index, or a blended 20/80 benchmark — to compare against. Without benchmark data, the engine cannot compute relative performance metrics like alpha, tracking error, or information ratio, which are standard measures for evaluating whether a portfolio strategy adds value. Benchmark data was not included in the Vise-provided datasets because the project scope was focused on building the accounting and rebalancing infrastructure rather than on performance attribution. Our team accepted this limitation and reported returns in absolute terms only. Adding a benchmark comparison would require downloading a separate return series and aligning it to the same date range, which is a straightforward extension that does not require changes to the core engine architecture.

Individual Client Tax Bracket Data
The engine applies flat federal tax rates — 35% for short-term capital gains, 20% for long-term capital gains, and 15% for qualified dividends — to all simulated transactions. In reality, tax rates vary by client based on their income bracket, filing status, state of residence, and whether they are subject to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT). These client-specific tax parameters are unavailable because they constitute sensitive personal financial information that Vise cannot share, and the engine is not connected to any tax preparation system that would provide them. Our team used the hardcoded flat rates as a reasonable proxy for a high-income investor, which is the typical profile of a Vise client. The TAX_CONFIG dictionary in the engine is designed to be easily updated with client-specific rates when the engine is integrated into a production environment where that information is available.

Volume-Based Liquidity and Market Impact Modeling
The price dataset includes a VOLUME column that is loaded into the engine, type-enforced, and cleaned, but it is never referenced in any trade execution, position sizing, or cost estimation logic. The engine instead applies a flat slippage of 5 basis points to every trade regardless of the security's liquidity profile or the trade's size relative to average daily volume. This matters because large trades in illiquid ETFs can move the market and incur significantly higher execution costs than the flat assumption reflects. The volume data is present and available — it is simply not yet incorporated into the engine's cost model. Our team used the flat slippage assumption as a simplification for the current version, which is reasonable for the liquid, large-cap ETFs that dominate Vise's model portfolios. A future enhancement could use the VOLUME field to scale slippage dynamically — for example, applying higher slippage when a trade exceeds a threshold percentage of average daily volume.

Settlement Cycle Modeling (T+1)
In U.S. equity and ETF markets, trades settle on a T+1 basis, meaning the buyer does not officially own the shares and the seller does not receive cash until one business day after the trade date. The engine does not model settlement delays — all trades settle instantly, with cash and share balances updating on the trade date itself. This matters for scenarios where the engine schedules multiple trades in quick succession, as real-world settlement constraints could limit the availability of cash or shares for subsequent trades. Settlement cycle data is not a dataset Vise would provide; it is a market structure rule that would need to be implemented as business logic within the engine. Our team accepted this simplification for the current MVP, noting that for the daily rebalancing cadence the engine operates on, the one-day settlement lag is unlikely to produce materially different results than instant settlement. This is documented in the engine's architecture notes as a known simplification.

Duplicate and Inconsistent Column Names in Source Files
Both the dividend dataset and the splits dataset contain duplicate column names — SUPPLEMENTALTYPEID appears twice in the dividend file, and SPLITTYPEID appears twice in the splits file. Additionally, the second instance of SPLITTYPEID and the associated SPLITTYPENAME column in the splits file are entirely empty. These duplicates are artifacts of the source data export from Capital IQ and do not represent distinct data fields. Our team handled this by using explicit `usecols` parameters when loading CSV files into the engine, selecting only the columns needed for processing and avoiding the duplicate fields entirely. The engine's data loading code specifies `_price_cols` and `_div_cols` lists to control exactly which columns are imported, which prevents pandas from raising errors on duplicate column names and ensures only clean, relevant fields enter the processing pipeline.
